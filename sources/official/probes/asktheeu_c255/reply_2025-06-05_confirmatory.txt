B.     THE CONTEXT

8.   According to Article 255, first paragraph, of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union
     (hereinafter “TFEU”), “[a] panel shall be set up in order to give an opinion on candidates’
     suitability to perform the duties of Judge and Advocate-General of the Court of Justice and the
     General Court (…)”.

9.   The panel provided for in Article 255 TFEU (hereinafter “the panel”) - which is composed of
     seven persons chosen among former members of the Court of Justice of the European Union
     (hereinafter “CJEU”) and the General Court, members of national supreme courts and lawyers
     of recognised competence - provides its opinions to the intergovernmental conference that,
     according to Articles 253 and 254 TFEU, appoints the Judges and Advocates-General of the
     CJEU and the General Court by common accord.

10. The panel operates according to operating rules that have been laid down by Council Decision
     2010/124/EU of 25 February 2010 (OJ L 50, 27.02.2010, p. 18-19), in line with Article 255,
     second paragraph, TFEU. The operating rules establish a principle of confidentiality of the
     activity of the panel and one of limited circulation of the relevant panel documents (candidacies
     submitted to the panel and panel's opinions):

        i.   According to point five of the rules, “the deliberations of the panel shall take place in
             camera”;

      ii.    Point seven makes clear that “Except where a proposal relates to the reappointment of a
             Judge or Advocate-General, the panel shall hear the candidate; the hearing shall take
             place in private”;

      iii.   Point six provides that “As soon as a Government of a Member State proposes a candidate,
             the General Secretariat of the Council shall send that proposal to the President of the
             panel”;




                                                                                                    6
      v.    Point eight states that the reasons for the opinion given by the panel shall be stated and
            that “the statement of reasons shall set out the principal grounds on which the panel’s
            opinion is based”. It also determines that “The panel's opinion shall be forwarded to the
            Representatives of the Governments of the Member States”. Moreover, at the request of
            the Presidency, “the President of the panel shall present that opinion to the
            Representatives of the Governments of the Member States' meeting within the Council”.

11. The above provisions rule out that the assessments conducted by the panel are carried out in
     public or addressed to the public; on the contrary, they make clear that the panel's opinions are
     intended exclusively for the Member State governments in the context of the appointment
     procedure carried out by the intergovernmental conference.

12. Consequently, the panel's opinions are not proactively made accessible to the public. However,
     the Council stresses that the panel has taken significant steps to make the public at large more
     acquainted with its activities. In particular, its activity reports provide an overall view of the work
     carried out and a better understanding of its procedures. Those reports contain exhaustive
     statistical data from the period of reference on the number of panel meetings held and the time
     taken to assess candidates, the number of candidates assessed, and the nature of the opinions
     delivered, whether favourable or unfavourable, as well as the outcome of the opinions. They
     further elaborate in extenso on the procedures followed for assessing candidates proposed for
     renewal of their term of office or for a first term of office, the comprehensive information
     required of the governments and the candidates, and the criteria used for the assessment of the
     candidates’ suitability. The panel’s reports are also directly accessible on the panel’s website, but
     also from the Council's public register of documents and on the website of the CJEU 1.

19. The Council considers that the requested documents fall within the remit of the exceptions
     relating to the protection of the public interest as regards privacy and the integrity of the

     statements made by the applicant in relation to the relevance of the decision of the European
     Ombudsman in case 1955/2017/THH to the case at hand.

     1. The protection of the privacy and integrity of the individuals concerned

20. The Council considers that the requested opinions contain for most of their parts personal data
     pertaining to the two candidates to whom they refer 5. In particular, the Council considers that the
     notion of personal data manifestly covers both the factual elements concerning the candidates’
     professional experience as well as the qualifications, and the panel’s assessment of the
     candidates’ competences.

21. According to Article 3(1) of Regulation (EU) No 2018/1725 (OJ L 295, 21.11.2018, p. 39-98)
     (hereinafter “Regulation No 2018/1725”), personal data is defined as “any information relating
     to an identified or identifiable natural person”. Moreover, the CJEU has ruled that professional
     data or information provided as part of a professional activity must also be characterised as
     personal data 6; it has pointed out that objection or the agreement to disclosure is not a constituent
     part of the concept 7; it has further stressed that the fact that certain information has already been
     made public does not preclude its characterisation as personal data 8; finally, surnames and
     forenames, even alone, qualify as personal data 9.

23. According to established case law, where an applicant seeks to obtain access to a document that
     includes personal data within the meaning of Article 3(1) of Regulation (EU) 2018/1725, the
     provisions of that Regulation become applicable in their entirety 10. More specifically, pursuant
     to Article 9 of Regulation (EU) 2018/1725, “personal data shall only be transmitted to recipients
     established in the Union other than Union institutions and bodies if:

     […]

     (b) the recipient establishes that it is necessary to have the data transmitted for a specific
     purpose in the public interest and the controller, where there is any reason to assume that the
     data subject’s legitimate interests might be prejudiced, establishes that it is proportionate to
     transmit the personal data for that specific purpose after having demonstrably weighed the
     various competing interests.

     […]

     3. Union institutions and bodies shall reconcile the right to the protection of personal data with
     the right of access to documents in accordance with Union law.”.

     It follows from that provision that it is up to the applicant to show whether the transfer of the
     requested personal data is necessary – that is to say, whether it is the most appropriate measure
     to achieve the objective pursued by the applicant and if it is proportionate to that objective.
     However, such a disclosure should not prejudice disproportionally the legitimate interests of the
     individual or individuals concerned 11.

The necessity and proportionality of the transfer

24. The applicant argues that making the content of the opinions publicly available is in the public
     interest 12. Notably, the applicant argues that “the public interest arises from – and is linked to –
     the public debate prompted by unprecedented, EU-wide and intense public coverage and
     political contestation around the two specific 255 opinions of candidates as witnessed in EU
     media, national media, and specialised media”. The applicant asserts that because the designation
     of the candidates by the Portuguese government was public and announced in the media, “their
     non-confirmation automatically suggested that something went wrong”. The applicant concludes
     that the non-disclosure of the opinions would therefore only protect why and not who has
     received an unfavourable opinion.

25. In this regard, the Council finds necessary to note from the outset that the operating rules of the
     panel, set out in Council Decision 2010/124/EU, provide for confidentiality of the activity of the
     panel and for limited circulation of the panel’s opinions, as explained above. It is also further
     recalled that according to those rules, the panel’s opinions are exclusively intended for Member
     States governments. Therefore, the current legal framework excludes that the panel’s opinions
     are publicly disclosed and that a public debate takes place in relation to them. If any discussion
     in the public space takes place, as in the present case such a discussion of academic nature has
     taken place in relation to one of the opinions requested, this can be possible because the rules on
     circulation of the opinions are not respected and an opinion is leaked. The fact that such a
     discussion takes place is not a justification for asking the Council to jeopardize the confidentiality
     of the opinions.

26. In addition, the Council notes that it is the choice of the governments whether to make public or
     not the name of their candidates for the post of Judge or Advocate-General at the CJEU. If the
     name of a candidate has been made public at national level, this is not a reason either for the
     Council to disclose the panel’s opinion on that candidate in breach of the rules referred to above
     which provide for protection of both the identity of the candidate and the assessment of the
     candidacy. Finally, the “non-confirmation” to which the applicant refers may result from
     different reasons and it is ultimately a decision of the intergovernmental conference.

27. More specifically concerning the necessity to transfer in the public interest the personal data
     contained in the two opinions requested, it is to be noted that the applicant, in his confirmatory
     application, essentially relies on general arguments in favour of transparency. While the
     arguments in favour of transparency are a legitimate objective that the Council is carefully
     considering, the Council does not consider that the applicant’s arguments are sufficient to
     establish the necessity of transferring the requested personal data, in as much as he has not
     demonstrated why such transfer would be the most appropriate measure to achieve the objective
     pursued, nor that the transfer is proportionate to that objective, as required by the case-law 13.

28. It first needs to be reminded that the automatic prevalence of the principle of transparency over
     data protection has been expressly ruled out by the Court 14. Indeed, Regulation (EC) No
     1049/2001 only provides a right of public access to the extent that none of the exceptions
     provided for in that Regulation applies. While transparency is indeed essential for the democratic
     functioning of the EU, the right to access documents under Regulation (EC) No 1049/2001 is
     subject to limitations that, among others, safeguard personal data.

29. As regards the objective to ensure the public trust in the process for the appointment of the
     members of the EU judicature, the Council stresses that this is exactly the objective that led to
     the establishment of the panel in the first place and has inspired the panel’s operating rules, which
     provide for the confidentiality of its activities.

30. In that regard, the Council further stresses that if transparency is crucial to allow citizens to hold
     accountable the political decision-makers and therefore to strengthen the democratic legitimacy
     of the EU institutions that are representative in nature, it plays a very different role in relation to
     the EU judiciary. The legitimacy of the Judges and Advocates-General is first and foremost
     assured by their independence, objectivity and professional competence, not by the power of the
     public opinion. It is not for members of the public to assess the suitability of candidates to the
     post of Judge or Advocate-General. In this context, the disclosure of the personal data requested
     by the applicant would, for the reasons that will be set out in section D.2 below, risk
     compromising the effective selection of suitable candidates to the post of Judges and Advocates-
     General and would therefore undermine, rather than pursue, the objective of ensuring the public's
     trust in the selection process and in the EU courts.

The prejudice to legitimate interests of the candidates

32. The Council considers that the disclosure of the requested personal data would inevitably harm
     the reputation of the candidates, and thus prejudice their legitimate interests.

33. The demanding professional requirements associated with the post of Judge or Advocate-General
     at the EU courts usually attract individuals of a particularly high seniority and who often hold
     prominent positions, both at national and EU level, such as Judges in the highest courts or
     renowned professors. The reputation of individuals in such positions would inevitably suffer a
     greater damage should unfavourable opinions concerning them be made public. Such a
     reputational damage could even have an effect on their potential career prospects, both at national
     and international level, even if the opinion of the panel is on the suitability of the candidate to
     perform the functions of a Judge or Advocate-General in the CJEU or the General Court. Even
     in the case of favourable opinions, the assessment may contain remarks, observations or point to
     certain less solid elements in the candidates’ qualifications or profiles.

34. In this respect, it is to be reminded that the assessment considerations identified by the panel in
     its activity reports are broad and go well beyond the knowledge of EU law. Thus, the legal
     capabilities of the candidates for a first term of office are assessed on the basis of the written files
     of the candidates, as well as on the basis of the replies they provide to the panel in the hearing.
     As specified in the activity reports of the panel 15, the questions asked at the hearing serve, inter
     alia, to “assess candidates’ analytical abilities and capacity for reasoning with regard to the
     conditions and mechanisms for applying the law, particularly as regards the application of EU
     law”. From this it follows that the disclosure of any remarks identifying candidates’ weaknesses
     in relation to their analytical abilities and capacity for reasoning with regard to the conditions
     and mechanisms for applying the law may harm the reputation of the candidates.

35. In light of these considerations, the Council deems that the disclosure of the requested personal
     data would cause prejudice to the legitimate interests of the candidates and that, on balance, those
     interests prevail over the objectives pursued by the applicant. Contrary to what the applicant
     argues in its confirmatory application, the Council considers that it has sufficiently demonstrated

     undermine the right to privacy of the individuals concerned, and that the risk of this interest being
     undermined is reasonably foreseeable and not purely hypothetical.

36. Furthermore, the argument raised by the applicant according to which disclosure of the requested
     documents could not harm the reputation of the candidates and prejudice their legitimate interests
     in so far as the content of the opinions “have - amid unprecedented media coverage - previously
     been made available to the public”, must be refused. The Court has already found that the fact

     basis of the exception relating to the protection of the public interest as regards privacy and the
     integrity of the individual, provided for in Article 4 (1) (b) of Regulation (EC) No 1049/2001.

   3. The protection of the candidates’ commercial interests

50. The Council further considers that the disclosure of the requested opinions could undermine the
    protection of the candidates' commercial interests (Article 4(2) first indent of Regulation (EC)
    No 1049/2001), in the event that the candidates were to carry out paid work as lawyers or legal
    advisers.

51. While an opinion of the panel concerns the suitability of a candidate for a specific position, the
    fact remains that the evaluation carried out by the panel takes into account the file submitted by
    the candidate, their academic record, their professional experience and their performance at the
    hearing. All this information would be relevant for any other position, in either the public or the
    private sector, for which the candidate might later be considered, since it shows the capabilities
    of the candidate as a legal professional. As explained in point 34 above, in the case of candidates
    for a first term of office, at the hearing the panel assesses their “analytical abilities and capacity
    for reasoning with regard to the conditions and mechanisms for applying the law, particularly
    as regards the application of EU law”. Therefore, the disclosure of any remarks identifying
    candidates’ weaknesses in relation to their analytical abilities and capacity for reasoning with
    regard to the conditions and mechanisms for applying the law, in particular in the case of
    unfavourable opinions, could have a negative impact on the candidate’s chances of succeeding
    in other selection procedures.

52. Furthermore, the argument raised by the applicant, according to which disclosure of the requested
    documents could not undermine the protection of the candidates’ commercial interests, in so far
    as the contents of the opinions “have become the object of unprecedented media coverage and
    fuelled speculation that has damaged – not protected – the alleged candidates’ commercial
    interests”, must be refused. As already pointed out in point 36 above, the Court has already found
    that the fact that a document had already been leaked, cannot, in itself, have any bearing on the
    possibility for the institution to rely on one of the exceptions provided for under Regulation (EC)
    No 1049/2001.

   4. The overriding public interest in disclosure

54. When an institution determines that one of the interests protected by the exceptions provided for
    in Article 4(2) and (3) of Regulation (EC) No 1049/2001 would be undermined by disclosure, it
    must assess whether there is an overriding public interest that would nonetheless justify making
    the requested documents available.

55. In this case, the applicant’s position is that the lack of transparency, particularly regarding the
    unfavourable opinions of the panel, creates an environment of speculation and manipulation that
    damages candidates' reputations. The applicant argues that the current practice of keeping these
    opinions confidential undermines the legitimacy of the selection process and promotes public
    distrust.

56. The Council considers that as regards the existence of an overriding public interest in disclosure,
    on balance, the principle of transparency which underpins Regulation (EC) No 1049/2001 would
    not, in this case, prevail over the abovementioned interests protected under the exceptions laid
    down in Article 4(2) and (3) of Regulation (EC) No 1049/2001 in such a way as to justify
    disclosure of the documents.

57. The Council acknowledges that transparency, and more specifically legislative transparency,
    plays a critical role in the functioning of the EU democratic system, as enshrined in the Treaties,
    secondary legislation and relevant case-law.

58. However, the Council deems here useful to refer to the considerations that it has developed in
    points 28-31 of this reply and to underline once more that in a democratic society, transparency
    and public participation do not have the same role in relation to legislative activity and in respect
    of the appointment and the role of the judiciary.

59. Magistrates are subject only to the law. Their position cannot be compared to that of politicians
     or citizens’ representatives. As a consequence, the procedure for their appointment needs to strike
     a balance between the need to select the candidates with the best legal expertise, the greatest
     professional experience and guarantees of integrity and independence, and the principle of
     transparency.

60. The Council is of the view, in this regard, that such a balance is satisfied by the significant level
     of transparency that is already ensured through the panel’s periodic publication of detailed
     activity reports, which as explained above provide information about its working methods, its
     criteria for assessing candidates and its overall yearly activity.

61. When it comes to the publication of individual opinions on the suitability of candidates for the
     post of Judge or Advocate-General, the Council believes that, on balance, the public interest in
     having access to those opinions does not override the interests in the protection of the decision-
     making process and the candidates' commercial interests.

62. The Council would therefore like to conclude that there is no overriding public interest that would
     justify fully disclosing the requested documents.
